Translation guide and terminology

Practice the handoff

Allow about 15 minutes. The evidence comes from a lab run with simulated requests. You can speak or write your answers.

With a partner, choose a responder and a teammate who will receive the handoff. Give the newer responder time to answer first. Switch roles in round 2. If you work at different times, exchange written responses after each round.

On your own, say or write your first response before reading the next round. In round 2, answer as the teammate receiving the handoff. You can pause between rounds or ask someone for help.

Round 1. What changed for people using the service?

Two checks estimated the service’s p95 request time. The first estimate was about 24 milliseconds (ms). The second was about 483 ms. Each check used requests from the previous 30 seconds.

Imagine 100 requests in that second window. Roughly 95 finished in 483 ms or less. The service groups request times into ranges, so the p95 value is an estimate.

Give your teammate one sentence about the slower requests. What would you still want to know about someone trying to complete a task?

A detector learned how the service’s measurements usually change together. Before looking at the scores, predict whether it will flag the change. If you need another measurement to decide, name it and explain why.

Round 2. What does the detector add?

The detector uses principal component analysis (PCA). It learned a pattern from an earlier set of measurements called the baseline. Its score measures how far a new combination of measurements falls from that pattern. The score has no time units.

The detector flagged scores above a threshold of about 1.102. The biggest contributor is the measurement that accounts for the largest part of the difference from the learned pattern.

Switch roles now. The new responder names what to investigate first and one other explanation that fits the readings. The teammate asks, “What could you check next, and what result would make you call someone now?” On your own, answer both parts.

Round 3. Give the next responder a handoff

Give your handoff before opening the facilitator guide. You can use these sentences to start.

  • “I observed ___. This suggests ___.”
  • “I still need to find out ___.”
  • “I’ll ask ___ to check ___. If they find ___, I would ___.”

Would you use every future threshold crossing to wake the person on call? Explain your decision. What evidence would change it?

After you decide, open the guide below. Compare your handoff with the examples. Choose one phrase to keep and one to revise. You can edit your notes here.

Facilitator guide and feedback

Open the guide to review the exercise

Prompts for each round

Offer speaking or writing and let the learner set the pace. Give the newer responder the first turn. A successful handoff gives the next person enough information to act. Naming the cause is optional.

  1. Ask what someone waiting for a slower request might notice. If the learner says every request took 483 ms, ask how they would describe the second reading using 100 requests. Let them revise their answer.
  2. Switch roles at the score table. Ask which reading describes a wait in milliseconds and whether it tells us one person’s wait. A dependency could be slow, or requests could be waiting inside the app. What check would distinguish those explanations?
  3. Receive the handoff. Ask which task to check, over which time interval, and what result would change the response. Give the learner time to decide before discussing the recording.

What happened in the recording?

The experimenter added 300 ms of delay to the app for about 33 seconds while simulated requests continued. Then they removed the delay. The detector flagged all seven samples labeled with the fault. Those seven samples describe one slowdown.

The 24 ms and 483 ms estimates came from separate checks of the service’s latency histogram. Each used a 30-second window. The detector’s request-time measurements used one-minute windows.

Compare two possible handoffs

“P95 request time rose from about 24 to 483 ms. I don’t know whether people can complete the main task now. I’ll ask the service owner to check recent completion rates and response times for that task. If it is still slow or failing, I’ll call the on-call lead.”

“The slower requests took longer, and request time contributed most when the detector flagged the change. I’ll ask my on-call partner for help now. Together we’ll check whether people can complete the main task. If completions have dropped, we’ll involve the service owner.”

One responder asks the service owner to verify impact. The other brings in a partner immediately to help check it. Ask what evidence and urgency informed the learner’s choice.

Give feedback on the handoff

Quote one phrase from the learner’s handoff. Use these questions to invite one specific revision. Working alone, read your revised handoff as the person receiving it.

  • Evidence. Which measurement changed, by how much, and in what units?
  • Uncertainty. What still needs checking about the cause, current recovery, or people’s ability to complete a task?
  • Action. Who will check what, and which finding would prompt them to call for help?

Discuss the paging decision

One possible decision is to keep the score on the dashboard for now. The detector caught one slowdown. Only two normal samples were reserved for evaluation. Before using it to wake someone, check a longer healthy period and ordinary deployments for unnecessary alerts. Replay other incidents to see whether it gives responders enough time to help.

Ask the learner to name one comparison they would make and one cost they would consider, such as waking someone during a healthy deployment or missing a slowdown that stops people from completing a task.
